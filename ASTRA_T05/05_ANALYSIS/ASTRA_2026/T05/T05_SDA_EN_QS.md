## Part A: Ancient and Medieval History

**1.** Bronze, which is harder than copper, is an alloy of
a) copper and zinc
b) iron and tin
c) copper and iron
d) copper and tin

**Correct Option: (d)**

**Justification:**
**Option (a): Incorrect.** Bronze is made by adding **tin**, not zinc, to copper (DSERT Class 6 Social Science Part 1, 2026-27, Ch 1, PDF p. 28).
**Option (b): Incorrect.** Bronze has copper as its base metal; tin was added to copper, not to iron (DSERT Class 6 Social Science Part 1, 2026-27, Ch 1, PDF p. 28).
**Option (c): Incorrect.** Iron is not a component of bronze. The textbook treats iron as a separate, very hard metal that marks a later stage, the Iron Age (DSERT Class 6 Social Science Part 1, 2026-27, Ch 1, PDF p. 28).
**Option (d): Correct.** Early people learned to add **tin** to **copper** and so produced bronze, an alloy harder than copper (DSERT Class 6 Social Science Part 1, 2026-27, Ch 1, PDF p. 28).
Hence the correct answer is option (d).

**Result:** Bronze = copper + tin, and it is harder than copper.

**Salient Features: The coming of metals**
The Karnataka textbook describes the move from stone to metal as gradual, with stone tools staying in use alongside the new metals.
- **Copper first:** the use of metals began towards the end of the Neolithic period, and copper was the first metal that human beings used (DSERT Class 6 Social Science Part 1, 2026-27, Ch 1, PDF p. 28).
- **"Copper and bronze stone age":** copper and bronze weapons were few, so stone weapons continued alongside them, which is why the period carries this name (DSERT Class 6 Social Science Part 1, 2026-27, Ch 1, PDF p. 28).
- **Iron and production:** iron weapons and tools aided agriculture and handicraft production (DSERT Class 6 Social Science Part 1, 2026-27, Ch 1, PDF p. 28).
- **Three periods of history:** history is generally classified into the pre-historic, proto-historic and historic periods (DSERT Class 6 Social Science Part 1, 2026-27, Ch 1, PDF p. 24).

**Exam Fact:** The pre-historic period makes up 99.9 per cent of human history, yet few sources survive to study it (DSERT Class 6 Social Science Part 1, 2026-27, Ch 1, PDF p. 24).

**2.** Consider the following statements regarding the literary contributions of the Satavahana period:
1. The Gatha Saptasati, an anthology of Prakrit love lyrics, is traditionally attributed to the Satavahana king Hala.
2. Gunadhya, associated with the Satavahana court, composed the Brihatkatha, a collection of tales, in the Paishachi Prakrit dialect.
3. Sarvasena composed the Harivijaya as a Sanskrit mahakavya.
4. The Satavahanas used Sanskrit as their exclusive official language, and avoided Prakrit in their inscriptions.
Which of the above statements are correct?
a) 1, 3 and 4 only
b) 1 and 2 only
c) 1, 2, 3 and 4
d) 1, 2 and 3 only

**Correct Option: (b)**

**Justification:**
**Statement 1: Correct.** Prakrit literature flourished in this period, and the Gata Sattasati, a collection of Prakrit verses, is "supposed to have been composed by Hala" (Kamath, A Concise History of Karnataka, p. 28). II PUC also names Hala as the Satavahana king who wrote the Gathasapthashati in Prakrit (II PUC History, Ch 4.3, PDF p. 60).
**Statement 2: Correct.** The Brihatkatha of Gunadhya, a work of romantic tales, was written in Paisachi Prakrit during the Satavahana period (Kamath, p. 28).
**Statement 3: Incorrect.** Sarvasena's Harivijaya is a Prakrit kavya, not a Sanskrit mahakavya. The statement swaps the language.
**Statement 4: Incorrect.** Prakrit was the official language of the Shatavahanas, and all their inscriptions are in Prakrit (Kamath, p. 27). R.S. Sharma notes that inscriptions in the peninsula from the second century BC to the third century AD were mostly in Prakrit, and that Sanskrit became the official language there only from about AD 400 (R.S. Sharma, Ancient India, p. 156).
Statements 1 and 2 are correct, which is option (b). Option (a) omits the true statement 2 and includes the false 3 and 4; option (c) includes the false 3 and 4; option (d) includes the false statement 3.
Hence the correct answer is option (b).

**Result:** Hala's Gata Sattasati and Gunadhya's Brihatkatha are Prakrit works; the Harivijaya is Prakrit too, and the Satavahanas' official language was Prakrit, not Sanskrit.

**Salient Features: Letters in the Satavahana age**
The Satavahana period is remembered for Prakrit literature, and some of it survives only through later hands.
- **The Brihatkatha's survival:** the work is known through its later Sanskrit translations by Kshemendra and Somadeva (Kamath, p. 28).
- **A Ganga translator:** the Ganga king Durvinita translated Gunadhya's Vaddakatha from Prakrit into Sanskrit (Kamath, p. 54).
- **Sanskrit grammar too:** Sarva Varman, author of the Sanskrit grammar Katantra Vyakarana, was patronised by a Satavahana monarch (Kamath, p. 28).
- **Kannada traces:** many scholars recognise Kannada words in Hala's Gathasaptashati and surmise that Kannada was developing as a language in this period (Karnataka Gazetteer 1982, Part 1, p. 217).

**Exam Fact:** The Gata Sattasati is a collection of 700 Prakrit verses in the Arya metre (Kamath, A Concise History of Karnataka, p. 28).

**3.** Arrange the following in chronological order:
1. Shah Jahan has the ruby-studded Peacock Throne made.
2. Sher Shah introduces the silver rupiya and the copper dam.
3. Raja Todarmal serves as Akbar's minister of finance.

Select the correct answer using the code given below:
a) 3-2-1
b) 2-1-3
c) 1-3-2
d) 2-3-1

**Correct Option: (d)**

**Justification:**
The three events follow the order of the rulers concerned: Sher Shah, then Akbar, then Shah Jahan.
- **2. Sher Shah's coins:** Sher Shah introduced a new copper coin, the **dam**, and a silver coin, the **rupiya** (DSERT Class 9 Social Science Part 2, 2026-27, Ch 17, PDF p. 7). Sher Shah, the Afghan chieftain, had defeated Humayun, who fled to Persia and returned only after Afghan rule weakened (DSERT Class 6 Social Science Part 2, 2026-27, Ch 15, PDF p. 70). So Sher Shah's rule came before Akbar's.
- **3. Todarmal under Akbar:** Raja Todarmal was Akbar's famous minister of finance (DSERT Class 9 Social Science Part 2, 2026-27, Ch 17, PDF p. 9). Akbar ruled from 1556 to 1605 CE (DSERT Class 6 Social Science Part 2, 2026-27, Ch 15, PDF p. 81).
- **1. Shah Jahan's Peacock Throne:** Jahangir ruled after Akbar, and Shah Jahan became king after the death of his father Jahangir. Shah Jahan had the ruby-studded Peacock Throne made (DSERT Class 9 Social Science Part 2, 2026-27, Ch 17, PDF p. 10).

The correct sequence is therefore 2-3-1.
**Option (a): Incorrect.** 3-2-1 places Akbar's minister before Sher Shah, whose rule came earlier.
**Option (b): Incorrect.** 2-1-3 places Shah Jahan before Akbar, his grandfather.
**Option (c): Incorrect.** 1-3-2 reverses the whole order.
**Option (d): Correct.** 2-3-1 follows Sher Shah, Akbar and Shah Jahan in turn.
Hence the correct answer is option (d).

**Result:** Sher Shah's rupiya, then Akbar's Todarmal, then Shah Jahan's Peacock Throne.

**Salient Features: Rulers and their courts**
The Karnataka textbooks link each ruler in this period with a reform, a court figure or a monument.
- **Sher Shah and the farmer:** land was classified as good, average or bad according to its fertility, and if troop movements damaged crops, the government compensated the farmers (DSERT Class 9 Social Science Part 2, 2026-27, Ch 17, PDF p. 7).
- **Akbar's accession:** Akbar won the Second Battle of Panipat in 1556 against Hemu (DSERT Class 9 Social Science Part 2, 2026-27, Ch 17, PDF p. 7).
- **Dara Shukoh:** the Mughal prince was an extraordinary scholar who translated the Bhagavadgita (DSERT Class 6 Social Science Part 2, 2026-27, Ch 15, PDF p. 74).
- **Fatehpur Sikri:** the entrance to its Jami Masjid is the Buland Darwaza (DSERT Class 6 Social Science Part 2, 2026-27, Ch 15, PDF p. 75).

**Exam Fact:** Under Akbar's revenue system, land was classified by its tillage as polaj, parauti, chachar and banjar (DSERT Class 9 Social Science Part 2, 2026-27, Ch 17, PDF p. 9).

**4.** Which of the following Ashokan inscriptions found in Karnataka is significant because it mentions his personal name 'Ashoka', and not merely his titles?
a) Koppal edict
b) Brahmagiri edict
c) Maski edict
d) Sannati edict

**Correct Option: (c)**

**Justification:**
**Option (a): Incorrect.** Koppal district has Ashokan edicts at Gavimath and Palkigundu (II PUC History, Ch 4.3, PDF p. 54), but Koppal is not among the edicts that name Ashoka. Kamath names the Maski and Nittur edicts as the ones that mention the name of Ashoka (Kamath, p. 21), and the Handbook names Maski, Nittur and Udegolam (Handbook of Karnataka 2020, p. 116).
**Option (b): Incorrect.** Brahmagiri, in Chitradurga district, is an Ashokan edict site (II PUC History, Ch 4.3, PDF p. 56), but it is not among the edicts that name the king.
**Option (c): Correct.** The edicts referred to the king as 'Devanampriya' or 'Priyadarshi', which left historians in doubt about who issued them. The Maski edict refers to him as 'Devanampriya Ashokasa', which confirmed that Devanampriya Priyadarshi was Ashoka himself (II PUC History, Ch 4.3, PDF p. 56). The Maski edict was the first to be discovered bearing the king's name (Karnataka Gazetteer 1982, Part 1, p. 174).
**Option (d): Incorrect.** Sannati, in Kalaburagi district, is also an Ashokan edict site (II PUC History, Ch 4.3, PDF p. 56), but it is not among the edicts that name the king.
Hence the correct answer is option (c).

**Result:** The Maski edict calls the king 'Devanampriya Ashokasa', and so settled that Devanampriya Priyadarshi was Ashoka.

**Salient Features: Ashokan edicts in Karnataka**
Karnataka holds an unusually rich set of Ashoka's minor edicts.
- **The sites:** Maski in Raichur district; Gavimath and Palkigundu in Koppal district; Brahmagiri, Siddapura and Jatinga-Rameshwara in Chitradurga district; Nittur and Udegolam in Ballari district; and Sannati in Kalaburagi district (II PUC History, Ch 4.3, PDF pp. 54–56).
- **Later finds:** four more minor edicts were discovered in 1978–79 in Bellary district, and the Udegolam edict uses a danda as a punctuation mark (Karnataka Gazetteer 1982, Part 1, p. 174).
- **Literacy:** the Handbook describes these edicts as the earliest evidence of the spread of literacy in Karnataka (Handbook of Karnataka 2020, p. 116).
- **Many named edicts:** the Gazetteer notes that so many edicts bearing Ashoka's name have come to light from Karnataka (Karnataka Gazetteer 1982, Part 1, p. 174).

**Exam Fact:** The Maski edict of Ashoka was discovered in 1915 (Karnataka Gazetteer 1982, Part 1, p. 174).

**6.** Consider the following statements:

Statement I: In the Battle of Saraighat, the Ahoms defeated the Mughals.
Statement II: The Battle of Saraighat was fought after the Battle of Plassey.

Which one of the following is correct in respect of the above statements?
a) Both Statement I and Statement II are correct, and Statement II is the correct explanation of Statement I
b) Both Statement I and Statement II are correct, but Statement II is not the correct explanation of Statement I
c) Statement I is correct, but Statement II is incorrect
d) Statement I is incorrect, but Statement II is correct

**Correct Option: (c)**

**Justification:**
**Statement I: Correct.** In the Battle of Saraighat (1671), the Ahoms defeated the Mughals (NCERT Class 8 Exploring Society Part 1, Ch 2, PDF p. 3). It was fought on the Brahmaputra near present-day Guwahati, and the Ahom commander Lachit Borphukan led the Ahom forces (NCERT Class 8 Exploring Society Part 1, Ch 2, PDF p. 28).
**Statement II: Incorrect.** The Battle of Plassey, a British victory, was fought in **1757** (NCERT Class 8 Exploring Society Part 1, Ch 4, PDF pp. 3, 11). Saraighat (1671) was fought about 86 years **before** Plassey, not after it.
Since Statement II is incorrect, the question of explanation does not arise.
Hence the correct answer is option (c).

**Result:** Saraighat (1671, an Ahom victory) came before Plassey (1757).

**Salient Features: The Ahoms of Assam**
The Ahoms ruled in the Brahmaputra valley and resisted expansion into the Northeast in both the Sultanate and the Mughal periods (NCERT Class 8 Exploring Society Part 1, Ch 2, PDF p. 28).
- **Origins:** the Ahoms descended from the Shan (Tai) community and migrated from Thailand and Myanmar to Assam in the early 13th century. Sukaphaa founded the dynasty in 1228, and its first capital was Charaideo (DSERT Class 9 Social Science Part 2, 2026-27, Ch 17, PDF p. 3).
- **Paik system:** every able-bodied man served the state through labour or military duty in exchange for land rights (NCERT Class 8 Exploring Society Part 1, Ch 2, PDF p. 28).
- **Saraighat odds:** Lachit Borphukan and his 10,000 men defeated a Mughal force of 30,000 soldiers (NCERT Class 8 Exploring Society Part 1, Ch 2, PDF pp. 28–29).
- **The last struggle:** in 1682, under Gadadhar Singha, the last battle between the Ahoms and the Mughals took place, and the Mughal army was expelled from the Brahmaputra valley (DSERT Class 9 Social Science Part 2, 2026-27, Ch 17, PDF p. 5).

**Exam Fact:** "Borphukan" was the title of the commander-in-chief of the Ahom army, which is why Lachit is called Lachit Borphukan (DSERT Class 9 Social Science Part 2, 2026-27, Ch 17, PDF p. 4).

**7.** Which of the following mahajanapadas were not monarchies, but took important decisions in their sabha or samiti by discussion and, if necessary, by vote?
a) Magadha and Avanti
b) Magadha and Kosala
c) Kosala and Vatsa
d) Vajji and Malla

**Correct Option: (d)**

**Justification:**
**Option (a): Incorrect.** Magadha and Avanti were among the most powerful monarchies, where the raja was the ultimate authority and his position was usually hereditary (NCERT Class 7 Exploring Society Part 1, Ch 4, PDF p. 6).
**Option (b): Incorrect.** Magadha and Kosala were ruled by kings (DSERT Class 6 Social Science Part 1, 2026-27, Ch 5, PDF p. 101).
**Option (c): Incorrect.** Kosala and Vatsa were also famous mahajanapadas ruled by kings (DSERT Class 6 Social Science Part 1, 2026-27, Ch 5, PDF p. 101).
**Option (d): Correct.** In **Vajji** (Vriji) and the neighbouring **Malla**, the sabha or samiti took important decisions through discussion and, if necessary, through vote. These ganas or sanghas were not monarchies (NCERT Class 7 Exploring Society Part 1, Ch 4, PDF p. 6).
Hence the correct answer is option (d).

**Result:** Vajji and Malla were gana-sanghas run by assemblies; Magadha, Kosala, Avanti and Vatsa were monarchies.

**Salient Features: Ganas and sanghas**
The ganas or sanghas show that not every mahajanapada was a monarchy.
- **Vajji:** the most important of the republics (ganarajya). It was a union of many republics, with Vaishali in Bihar as its capital (DSERT Class 6 Social Science Part 1, 2026-27, Ch 5, PDF p. 101).
- **The sabha:** Vajji's sabha had elder and younger members, who were addressed as Raja. All matters of the union were discussed at its meetings (DSERT Class 6 Social Science Part 1, 2026-27, Ch 5, PDF p. 101).
- **Choosing the ruler:** in these ganas, the assembly even selected the raja, which is why scholars often call them "early republics" (NCERT Class 7 Exploring Society Part 1, Ch 4, PDF pp. 6–7).
- **Magadha's age:** the Buddha and Mahavira lived in the time of King Ajatashatru of Magadha (NCERT Class 7 Exploring Society Part 1, Ch 5, PDF p. 12).

**Exam Fact:** The first Indian coins were made of silver, into which symbols could be 'punched', and so they are called punch-marked coins (NCERT Class 7 Exploring Society Part 1, Ch 4, PDF pp. 7–8).

**8.** In the context of the prehistoric and proto-historic periods, which of the following statements are correct?
1. The Indus Valley Civilisation belongs to the historic period, as its script can be read.
2. Signs of ashes found in the caves of Kurnool show that Stone Age people knew the use of fire.
3. The people of Mehrgarh cultivated barley and wheat and raised sheep and goats.
4. The very small weapons called "delicate stone weapons" belong to the Middle Stone Age.

Select the correct answer using the code given below:
a) 2, 3 and 4 only
b) 1 and 2 only
c) 1, 3 and 4 only
d) 2 and 3 only

**Correct Option: (a)**

**Justification:**
**Statement 1: Incorrect.** The Indus Valley Civilisation belongs to the **proto-historic** period, which has written documents along with archaeological ruins that **cannot be read**. The historic period, with readable records, starts from the Vedic civilisation (DSERT Class 8 Social Science Part 1, 2026-27, Ch 2, PDF p. 25).
**Statement 2: Correct.** Signs of ashes found in the caves of Kurnool show that people of the Stone Age knew and used fire (DSERT Class 8 Social Science Part 1, 2026-27, Ch 2, PDF p. 26).
**Statement 3: Correct.** The people of Mehrgarh cultivated barley and wheat and raised sheep and goats (DSERT Class 8 Social Science Part 1, 2026-27, Ch 3, PDF p. 32).

**Salient Features: Reading the Stone Age record**
The textbook classifies history by the kind of evidence that survives.
- **Pre-historic period:** only archaeological ruins are available to reconstruct it, as with the Stone Age cultures (DSERT Class 8 Social Science Part 1, 2026-27, Ch 2, PDF p. 25).
- **Hunter-gatherer sites:** relics of hunting and food-gathering people have been found at Bhimbetka, Hunasagi and Kurnool (DSERT Class 8 Social Science Part 1, 2026-27, Ch 2, PDF p. 25).
- **Residential and industrial sites:** people settled near rivers, lakes and good stone. Heaps of stone chips left from tool-making mark these sites (DSERT Class 8 Social Science Part 1, 2026-27, Ch 2, PDF p. 26).
- **Stone Age stages:** the Middle Stone Age runs from about 12,000 to about 10,000 years ago, and the New Stone Age began after 10,000 years ago with bright, sharp-bladed weapons (DSERT Class 8 Social Science Part 1, 2026-27, Ch 2, PDF p. 28).

**Exam Fact:** Numismatics is the study of the evolution, nature and development of coins (DSERT Class 8 Social Science Part 1, 2026-27, Ch 1, PDF p. 18).

b) Muhammad bin Tughlaq

**10.** The Mathura school of art, unlike the Gandhara school, is distinguished by its use of which material and stylistic influence?
a) Grey schist, with strong Greco-Roman influence
b) White marble, with Persian influence
c) Red sandstone, with a largely indigenous Indian style
d) Black basalt, with Egyptian influence

**Correct Option: (c)**

**Justification:**
**Option (a): Incorrect.** Grey stone with Greco-Roman influence describes the Gandhara style: its sculptures were mostly crafted from grey-black schist, blending Greco-Roman elements with Indian features (NCERT Class 7, Exploring Society Part 1, Ch 6, p. 137).
**Option (b): Incorrect.** Neither school is described as working in white marble under Persian influence. Mathura used red sandstone (NCERT Class 7, Exploring Society Part 1, Ch 6, p. 140).
**Option (c): Correct.** Unlike the Gandhara style, the Mathura style primarily used red sandstone and reflects less influence from Greco-Roman aesthetics; it is known for its distinct Indian style (NCERT Class 7, Exploring Society Part 1, Ch 6, p. 140). R.S. Sharma calls Mathura primarily a centre of indigenous art, whose red sandstone products are found even outside Mathura (R.S. Sharma, Ancient India, p. 110).
**Option (d): Incorrect.** Black basalt with Egyptian influence matches neither school.
Hence the correct answer is option (c).

**Result:** Mathura: red sandstone and an indigenous style; Gandhara: grey schist and Greco-Roman influence.

**Salient Features: The Gandhara and Mathura schools**
Both schools flourished in the early centuries CE, but they drew on different traditions.
- **Origin of Gandhara art:** Indo-Greek rule introduced Hellenistic art features in the north-west frontier of India, giving rise to Gandhara art (R.S. Sharma, p. 104).
- **Gandhara features:** the Buddha's hair was fashioned in the Graeco-Roman style (R.S. Sharma, p. 110), and sculptors produced fine Buddha images with realistic anatomy and flowing robes (NCERT Class 7, Exploring Society Part 1, Ch 6, p. 137).
- **Mathura subjects:** Kubera, Lakshmi, Shiva, the Buddha, yakshas and yakshinis, generally as fuller figures with smooth modelling (NCERT Class 7, Exploring Society Part 1, Ch 6, p. 140), and also stone images of Vardhamana Mahavira (R.S. Sharma, p. 110).
- **The Mathura collection:** according to R.S. Sharma, the Mathura Museum possesses the largest collection of Kushan-period sculptures in India (R.S. Sharma, p. 110).

**Exam Fact:** Mathura is famous for the headless erect statue of Kanishka, whose name is inscribed on its lower part (R.S. Sharma, Ancient India, p. 110).

**11.** Consider the following statements about lesser-known Adil Shahi structures at Vijayapura (Bijapur):
1. Gagan Mahal, built in 1561, served as an audience hall with an arch nearly 62 feet wide.
2. Mehtar Mahal is a full-sized congregational mosque built for the queen of Ali Adil Shah I.
3. Anand Mahal, a palace attributed to Ibrahim Adil Shah II, is noted for its richly ornamented ceiling.
4. Sat Manzil, built by Ibrahim Adil Shah II, was a mausoleum with seven-storeyed minarets.
Which of the statements given above are correct?
a) 1 and 3 only
b) 1 and 2 only
c) 2 and 4 only
d) 1, 2, 3 and 4

**Correct Option: (a)**

**74.** Which of the following methods of assessing Mughal land revenue involved actual division of the harvested crop between the state and the cultivator, rather than a cash assessment based on measurement?
a) Nasaq
b) Zabti
c) Batai (Ghalla-Bakshi)
d) Dahsala

**Correct Option: (c)**

**Justification:**
**Option (a): Incorrect.** The nature of nasaq is debated: Moreland called it group assessment, while Irfan Habib considers it an estimation based on the previous assessment, whether that had been made by zabt, batai or another method (Satish Chandra, Medieval India Part II, PDF p. 84). On either reading it is not a physical division of the harvest.
**Option (b): Incorrect.** Zabt was the system of measurement adopted by Sher Shah and continued under Akbar (Satish Chandra, Medieval India Part II, PDF p. 81); bamboo measuring sticks with iron hooks were used to measure the land (DSERT Class 9 Social Science Part 2, 2026-27, p. 7).
**Option (c): Correct.** The Ain describes batai, also called bhaoli, in which the crops are reaped and stacked and divided by agreement in the presence of the parties (NCERT Class 12 Themes in Indian History Part II, Ch 8, p. 215). Satish Chandra lists this crop-sharing in three types: bhaoli, khet batai (the fields divided after they were sown) and lang batai (the cut grain formed into heaps and divided) (Satish Chandra, Medieval India Part II, PDF p. 84).
**Option (d): Incorrect.** Under the dahsala, the Ten-Year rates proclaimed in 1579, the state demand was expressed as a cash rate based on local productivity and local prices (Satish Chandra, Medieval India Part II, PDF p. 82). It is a cash assessment, not a division of the crop.
Hence the correct answer is option (c).

**Result:** Batai divided the harvested crop between the state and the cultivator; zabt and dahsala fixed a cash demand on measured land, and nasaq worked from earlier assessments.

**Salient Features: Assessing land revenue under Akbar**
The Ain lists several methods of assessment and collection in use side by side (NCERT Class 12 Themes in Indian History Part II, Ch 8, p. 215).
- **Classes of land:** polaj was cultivated every year and never left fallow; parauti was left out of cultivation for a time to recover; chachar had lain fallow for three or four years; banjar was uncultivated for five years and more (NCERT Class 12 Themes in Indian History Part II, Ch 8, p. 214).
- **Kankut:** in Hindi, kan means grain and kut estimate; under kankut the crop was estimated, and in case of doubt cut and estimated in three lots, good, middling and inferior (NCERT Class 12 Themes in Indian History Part II, Ch 8, p. 215).
- **Where zabt ran:** the dahsala system based on measurement was introduced from Lahore to Allahabad and in Gujarat, Malwa and parts of Bihar and Multan, though Irfan Habib thinks it improbable that zabt covered the whole land in any province (Satish Chandra, Medieval India Part II, PDF p. 83).
- **Kashmir:** there the produce was computed in ass-loads (kharwar) and then divided (Satish Chandra, Medieval India Part II, PDF p. 84).

**Exam Fact:** According to the Ain, the produce of the three classes of land was averaged, and one-third of this medium produce was exacted as the royal dues (NCERT Class 12 Themes in Indian History Part II, Ch 8, p. 214).
