## Part A: Ancient and Medieval History

**1.** Consider the following statements about the later Satavahana rulers.
1. Vashishtiputra Pulumavi, son and successor of Gautamiputra Satakarni, ruled from about 130 to 159 CE
2. Vashishtiputra Pulumavi established a matrimonial (blood) relationship with the Western Kshatrapa Kardamaka dynasty, as recorded in a Kanheri inscription
3. The Nashik Prashasti, glorifying Gautamiputra Satakarni's achievements, was composed in the name of his mother, Gautami Balashri
4. The Satavahana capital remained fixed at Amaravati throughout the dynasty's history
Which of the above statements are correct?
a) 2, 3 and 4 only
b) 1, 2 and 3 only
c) 1 and 2 only
d) 1, 2, 3 and 4

**Correct Option: (b)**

**Justification:**
**Statement 1: Correct.** Gautamiputra Satakarni was succeeded by his son Vashishtiputra Pulumavi, who ruled from about 130 to 159 CE (Kamath, A Concise History of Karnataka, p. 24). R.S. Sharma gives c. 130–154 CE (R.S. Sharma, Ancient India, p. 114); either way the reign begins about 130 CE, so the approximate statement holds.
**Statement 2: Correct.** Vashishtiputra Pulumavi established a blood relationship with the Kardamakas, the Western Kshatrapa line of Rudradaman, as made known by the Kanheri record (Kamath, p. 24).
**Statement 3: Correct.** Gautamiputra Satakarni's achievements are recorded by his mother, Gautami Balashri, in the Nasik cave inscription (II PUC History, Ch 4.3, PDF p. 60).
**Statement 4: Incorrect.** The Satavahana capital was Pratishthana, modern Paithan in Maharashtra (DSERT Class 6 Social Science Part 1, 2026-27, p. 89). NCERT notes that the Satavahanas had different capitals at different times, the best known being Amaravati and Pratishthana (NCERT Class 7, Exploring Society Part 1, p. 124). The capital was never fixed at Amaravati throughout.
Statements 1, 2 and 3 are correct, which is option (b). Options (a) and (d) include the false statement 4; option (c) omits the true statement 3.
Hence the correct answer is option (b).

**Result:** Pulumavi followed Gautamiputra and allied with the Kardamakas (Kanheri record); the Nasik eulogy is Gautami Balashri's; the capital was Pratishthana (Paithan), not a fixed Amaravati.

**Salient Features: The Satavahanas**
The Satavahanas, once feudatories of the Mauryas, ruled the Deccan for about 400 years, and their records reach into Karnataka (II PUC History, Ch 4.3, PDF p. 60).
- **Founder and capital:** Simuka founded the dynasty, and Prathisthana (modern Paithan in Maharashtra) was its capital (II PUC History, Ch 4.3, PDF p. 60).
- **Gautamiputra Satakarni:** he defeated the Shakas and restruck the coins of the Shaka ruler Nahapana with his own name. He bore the title 'Tri Samudra Toya Pitavahana', he whose horses had drunk the water of three seas (DSERT Class 6 Social Science Part 1, 2026-27, p. 90).
- **Ptolemy's evidence:** the Greek geographer Ptolemy speaks of Baithana as the capital of Polemaios, which indicates Paithan as the capital of Sri Pulumavi (Kamath, p. 24).
- **Karnataka link:** coins of Vashishtiputra Pulumavi have been found in Chitradurga, and an inscription of his son Shiva Sri Pulumavi has been found at Banavasi in Uttara Kannada (Karnataka Gazetteer 1982, Part 1, p. 217).

**Exam Fact:** Coins of Yajnasri Satakarni have been found in plenty in Karnataka (Karnataka Gazetteer 1982, Part 1, p. 217).

**2.** The people of which age came to be called "Quartzite man", after the very hard stone they used for their tools?
a) Mesolithic age
b) Chalcolithic age
c) Neolithic age
d) Palaeolithic age

**Correct Option: (d)**

**Justification:**
**Option (a): Incorrect.** Mesolithic means **Middle Stone Age** (Greek *meso*, middle). Its tools were small, generally between half an inch and one inch, and its people were not the ones called "Quartzite men" (II PUC History, Ch 2, PDF p. 26).
**Option (b): Incorrect.** The Chalcolithic (copper–stone) phase belongs to the gradual move into the Metal Age, when metal and stone implements were used side by side (II PUC History, Ch 2, PDF p. 27). The name "Quartzite man" belongs to the Old Stone Age.
**Option (c): Incorrect.** Neolithic means **New Stone Age**. Neolithic people used other stones along with quartzite, and their weapons were chipped, ground, grooved and polished (II PUC History, Ch 2, PDF p. 26). The label "Quartzite man" was not given to them.
**Option (d): Correct.** Palaeolithic people used a very hard stone called **quartzite**, and so they were also known as **"Quartzite men"** (II PUC History, Ch 2, PDF p. 25).
Hence the correct answer is option (d).

**Result:** "Quartzite man" is the Palaeolithic (Old Stone Age) human, named after the very hard quartzite of his tools.

**Salient Features: The Stone Age names**
Each Stone Age is named from Greek, by its place in the sequence.
- **Palaeolithic:** from *paleo* (old) and *lithic* (stone), so the Old Stone Age (II PUC History, Ch 2, PDF p. 25).
- **Palaeolithic tools:** rough, undressed stones fitted to stick or bone handles (II PUC History, Ch 2, PDF p. 25).
- **Mesolithic and Neolithic:** *meso* means middle and *neo* means new, giving the Middle and the New Stone Age (II PUC History, Ch 2, PDF p. 26).
- **Metal Age:** the move from stone to metal was gradual, and the Metal Age is classified into the Copper, Bronze and Iron Ages (II PUC History, Ch 2, PDF p. 27).

**Exam Fact:** Lingasugur in Raichur district and the Ghataprabha river basin in Belagavi (Belgaum) district are listed as Palaeolithic sites in Karnataka (II PUC History, Ch 2, PDF p. 26).

**3.** The Arab traveller Al-Masudi described the Gurjara-Pratihara kingdom as 'al-Juzr' and referred to its king as 'Baura', even though the king he named had already died. 'Baura' was Al-Masudi's garbled rendering of which title, found inscribed on the coins of Mihira Bhoja?
a) Parama Bhattaraka
b) Adivaraha
c) Maharajadhiraja
d) Parameshvara

**Correct Option: (b)**

**Justification:**
**Option (a): Incorrect.** Parama Bhattaraka was a general imperial title. Gupta kings, for example, styled themselves paramesvara, maharajadhiraja and paramabhattaraka (R.S. Sharma, Ancient India, p. 140). It was not Bhoja's personal coin legend.
**Option (b): Correct.** Bhoja was a devotee of Vishnu and adopted the title 'Adivaraha', which has been found inscribed on some of his coins. Al-Masudi called the Gurjara-Pratihara kingdom al-Juzr (a corrupt form of Gurjara) and its king Baura, probably a mispronunciation of Adivaraha, although Bhoja had died by that time (Satish Chandra, History of Medieval India, PDF pp. 31–32).
**Option (c): Incorrect.** Maharajadhiraja was another standard sovereign title, used by the Guptas and by the Chalukya Pulakeshin II among others (II PUC History, Ch 4.7, PDF p. 87). It has no link with Vishnu and no likeness to "Baura".
**Option (d): Incorrect.** Parameshvara belongs to the same family of imperial titles (R.S. Sharma, p. 140). It is not the title found on Bhoja's coins.
Hence the correct answer is option (b).

**Result:** "Baura" is read as Adivaraha, the Vaishnava title on Mihira Bhoja's coins; the other three are general imperial titles.

**Salient Features: Mihira Bhoja and the Pratiharas**
Bhoja rebuilt the Pratihara empire, and his title outlived him in a foreign account.
- **Kanauj:** Bhoja rebuilt the empire and by about AD 836 had recovered Kanauj, which remained the Pratihara capital for almost a century (Satish Chandra, PDF p. 31).
- **The name:** he is sometimes called Mihir Bhoja, to distinguish him from Bhoja Paramara of Ujjain (Satish Chandra, PDF p. 31).
- **Pratihara dominance:** the Pratiharas dominated north India for over a hundred years, from the early ninth to the middle of the tenth century (Satish Chandra, PDF p. 31).
- **Al-Masudi's army figures:** he said the king's army had four divisions, with the army of the north facing the ruler of Multan, that of the south the Rashtrakutas and that of the east the Palas (Satish Chandra, PDF p. 32).

**Exam Fact:** According to Al-Masudi, the empire of Juzr had 1,80,000 villages, cities and rural areas and was about 2,000 km long and 2,000 km broad (Satish Chandra, History of Medieval India, PDF p. 32).

**4.** Consider the following statements about the conflict between the Marathas and the Mughals:
1. Shivaji was defeated by the Mughal general Jai Singh and had to sign the Treaty of Purandar, ceding 23 forts to the Mughals.
2. After the treaty, Shivaji visited Aurangzeb's court at Agra, was insulted there, and escaped with his son Sambhaji by hiding in baskets of sweets.
3. Sambhaji, who succeeded Shivaji as Chhatrapati, was captured, tortured and executed by Aurangzeb, after which the Mughals captured Raigad.
4. After Sambhaji, Rajaram became Chhatrapati and continued the resistance from Raigad, which never fell to the Mughals.
Which of the statements given above are correct?
a) 1, 2, 3 and 4
b) 1 and 2 only
c) 2, 3 and 4 only
d) 1, 2 and 3 only

**Correct Option: (d)**

**Justification:**
**Statement 1: Correct.** Aurangzeb sent Jai Singh against Shivaji, who had to concede defeat at Purandar and enter into a treaty (NCERT Class 8, Exploring Society Part 1, Ch 3, p. 66). Under the Treaty of Purandar (1665) Shivaji surrendered 23 forts to the Mughals (II PUC History, Ch 5.3, PDF p. 136; DSERT Class 9 Social Science Part 2, 2026-27, p. 13).
**Statement 2: Correct.** Persuaded by Jai Singh, Shivaji visited Aurangzeb's court, was insulted, and was put under house arrest. He and his son Sambhaji escaped hidden in big baskets of fruits and sweets (NCERT Class 8, Exploring Society Part 1, Ch 3, p. 67).
**Statement 3: Correct.** After Shivaji's death Sambhaji became Chhatrapati. Aurangzeb captured him, tortured him and executed him, and then captured Raigad, the Maratha capital (NCERT Class 8, Exploring Society Part 1, Ch 3, p. 69).
**Statement 4: Incorrect.** Rajaram did become Chhatrapati after Sambhaji, but Raigad had already fallen, and he fled to Gingee in present-day Tamil Nadu (NCERT Class 8, Exploring Society Part 1, Ch 3, p. 69). The base at Jinji became his refuge during Aurangzeb's war on the Marathas (Satish Chandra, Medieval India Part II, PDF p. 179).
Statements 1, 2 and 3 are correct, which is option (d). Option (a) includes the false statement 4; option (b) omits the true statement 3; option (c) omits the true statement 1 and includes the false 4.
Hence the correct answer is option (d).

**Result:** Purandar, the Agra escape and Sambhaji's execution followed by the fall of Raigad are all correct; Rajaram fought on from Gingee, not from Raigad.

**Salient Features: The Marathas after Shivaji**
The Mughal-Maratha war outlasted both Shivaji and Aurangzeb.
- **Coronation:** in 1674 Shivaji was crowned at Raigad (NCERT Class 8, Exploring Society Part 1, Ch 3, p. 62).
- **Rajaram's campaigns:** Rajaram, Sambhaji's younger brother, fought the Mughals again and recaptured Gujarat, Malwa, Khandesh, Berar and other forts (DSERT Class 9 Social Science Part 2, 2026-27, p. 15).
- **Tarabai:** after Rajaram died in 1700, his wife Tarabai made her 10-year-old son Shivaji II king and ruled on his behalf from 1700 to 1708 (DSERT Class 9 Social Science Part 2, 2026-27, p. 15).
- **Aurangzeb's failure:** Aurangzeb was unable to leave the Deccan and died without subduing the Marathas (NCERT Class 8, Exploring Society Part 1, Ch 3, p. 69).

**Exam Fact:** Chennammaji of Keladi gave shelter to the Maratha ruler Rajaram, who had revolted against Aurangzeb (II PUC History, Ch 5, PDF p. 155).

**5.** Match the Mahajanapadas in List I with their capital cities in List II:

| List I | List II |
|---|---|
| A. Vatsa | I. Taxila |
| B. Avanti | II. Kausambi |
| C. Gandhara | III. Ujjaini |
| D. Panchala | IV. Ahichhatra |

Choose the correct answer from the options given below:
a) A-II, B-III, C-I, D-IV
b) A-II, B-IV, C-I, D-III
c) A-III, B-II, C-I, D-IV
d) A-II, B-III, C-IV, D-I

**Correct Option: (a)**

**Justification:**
The correct pairing is A-II, B-III, C-I, D-IV: Vatsa–Kausambi, Avanti–Ujjaini, Gandhara–Taxila and Panchala–Ahichhatra. NCERT's map of early states and their capitals marks Kaushambi in Vatsa, Ujjayini in Avanti, Taxila in Gandhara and Ahichchhatra in Panchala (NCERT Class 12, Themes in Indian History Part 1, Ch 2, p. 30). The II PUC table gives Kampila for Panchala (II PUC History, Ch 4.3, PDF p. 51), but Kampila is not among the choices, and Ahichhatra is the only List II city that fits Panchala.
**Option (a): Correct.** It gives all four pairs correctly.

**Salient Features: Early states and their capitals**
NCERT's map of early states places a capital in each kingdom, and several of these cities stayed important long after.
- **Avanti in two parts:** Avanti was divided into two, with the northern part's capital at Ujjain and the southern part's at Mahishamati. Ujjain eventually surpassed Mahishamati (R.S. Sharma, Ancient India, p. 71).

- **Other capitals on the map:** Shravasti in Koshala, Varanasi in Kashi, Rajgir in Magadha and Champa in Anga (NCERT Class 12, Themes in Indian History Part 1, Ch 2, p. 30).
- **Later importance:** under the Mauryas, Taxila and Ujjayini were provincial centres, both situated on important long-distance trade routes (NCERT Class 12, Themes in Indian History Part 1, Ch 2, pp. 32–33).

**Exam Fact:** The kingdom of Anga covered the modern districts of Monghyr and Bhagalpur and had its capital at Champa (R.S. Sharma, Ancient India, p. 69).

**6.** Consider the following statements regarding the decipherment of ancient Indian scripts.
1. James Prinsep deciphered the Ashokan Brahmi script in 1838, after decades of work by earlier epigraphists.
2. The Kharoshthi script of the north-west was read with the help of bilingual Indo-Greek coins that gave royal names in both Greek and Kharoshthi letters.
3. William Jones, founder of the Asiatic Society, was the scholar who first deciphered the Brahmi script and read Ashoka's edicts.
Which of the above statements are correct?
a) 1 only
b) 1 and 3 only
c) 1 and 2 only
d) 1, 2 and 3

**Correct Option: (c)**

**Justification:**
**Statement 1: Correct.** Only after decades of painstaking investigation by several epigraphists was James Prinsep able to decipher Asokan Brahmi in 1838 (NCERT Class 12, Themes in Indian History Part 1, Ch 2, p. 46).
**Statement 2: Correct.** Kharosthi, the script of inscriptions in the north-west, was read through coins of the Indo-Greek kings (c. second–first centuries BCE), which carry the names of kings in Greek and Kharosthi (NCERT Class 12, Themes in Indian History Part 1, Ch 2, pp. 46–47).
**Statement 3: Incorrect.** The decipherment of Brahmi is credited to James Prinsep, an officer in the mint of the East India Company, in 1838 (NCERT Class 12, Themes in Indian History Part 1, Ch 2, pp. 28, 46). It was not the work of William Jones. The Asiatic Society (Bengal) was founded in 1784, more than fifty years before the decipherment (NCERT Class 12, Ch 2, p. 51).
Statements 1 and 2 are correct, which is option (c). Option (a) omits the true statement 2; options (b) and (d) include the false statement 3.
Hence the correct answer is option (c).

**Result:** Prinsep deciphered Asokan Brahmi in 1838, and Kharosthi was read through bilingual Indo-Greek coins; William Jones did not decipher Brahmi.

**Salient Features: Reading India's earliest scripts**
The two scripts of the earliest inscriptions were read in different ways.
- **Working backwards:** most scripts used for modern Indian languages descend from Brahmi. Scholars worked backwards from Bengali and Devanagari manuscripts, comparing their letters with older specimens (NCERT Class 12, Themes in Indian History Part 1, Ch 2, p. 46).
- **A wrong assumption:** scholars sometimes assumed the early inscriptions were in Sanskrit, although the earliest were in fact in Prakrit (NCERT Class 12, Ch 2, p. 46).
- **Languages of the Asokan inscriptions:** most were in Prakrit. Those in the north-west were in Aramaic and Greek, and some Prakrit inscriptions in the north-west were written in Kharosthi (NCERT Class 12, Ch 2, p. 32).
- **Piyadassi:** Prinsep found that most inscriptions mentioned a king called Piyadassi, meaning "pleasant to behold" (NCERT Class 12, Ch 2, p. 28).

**Exam Fact:** In the 1810s Colin Mackenzie collected over 8,000 inscriptions in Sanskrit and Dravidian languages (NCERT Class 12, Themes in Indian History Part 1, Ch 2, p. 51).

**7.** Consider the following statements about the Chola rulers and their achievements:
1. Rajaraja I built the Brihadishvara temple (Rajarajeshvaram) at Thanjavur.
2. Rajendra I founded a new capital, Gangaikonda Cholapuram, to commemorate his victorious campaign in north India.
3. The Uttaramerur inscriptions of the Chola king Parantaka I describe the election of village assembly members by the kudavolai (pot-ticket) method.
Which of the above statements are correct?
a) 1 and 2 only
b) 1, 2 and 3
c) 2 and 3 only
d) 1 and 3 only

**Correct Option: (b)**

**Justification:**
**Statement 1: Correct.** Rajaraja Chola was a powerful ruler and an able administrator, and the Brihadishvara temple at Thanjavur is his contribution (DSERT Class 6 Social Science Part 1, 2026-27, p. 114).
**Statement 2: Correct.** To commemorate his successful expedition to northern India, Rajendra Chola assumed the title Gangaikonda and built a new capital called Gangaikonda Cholapuram (DSERT Class 6 Social Science Part 1, 2026-27, p. 114; II PUC History, Ch 4, PDF p. 77).
**Statement 3: Correct.** The Uttaramerur inscriptions of Parantaka I, issued in 919 and 921 CE, describe Chola village administration (II PUC History, Ch 4, PDF p. 75). Members were elected by the kudavolai (lucky draw) system: the names of candidates were written on palm leaves and put in a pot (II PUC History, Ch 4, PDF p. 78).
All three statements are correct, which is option (b). Options (a), (c) and (d) each omit one true statement.
Hence the correct answer is option (b).

**Result:** Rajaraja I built the Thanjavur temple, Rajendra I built Gangaikonda Cholapuram, and Parantaka I's Uttaramerur inscriptions describe the pot-ticket election.

**Salient Features: Chola village self-government**
The Uttaramerur inscriptions record in detail how a Chola village chose its representatives.
- **Wards:** according to the inscription, Uttaramerur village was divided into thirty parts (kudumbu), and one representative was elected from each for a period of one year (II PUC History, Ch 4, PDF p. 78).
- **What the inscriptions record:** the election process, including sealed ballot boxes, the qualifications of members, their duties and the conditions that could lead to their dismissal (NCERT Class 7, Exploring Society Part 1, p. 197).
- **Learning:** Rajendra Chola I set up a centre of higher education at Ennayiram (South Arcot) in 1025 CE, with free boarding and lodging for 340 students (II PUC History, Ch 4, PDF p. 77).

**Exam Fact:** The committees of a Chola village, such as the Annual, Garden and Tank Bund committees, were called 'Variyam', and the elected representatives who worked in them were called 'Variya Perumakkal' (II PUC History, Ch 4, PDF p. 78).

**8.** With reference to Sultan Ghiyasuddin Balban of the Delhi Sultanate, consider the following statements:
1. He introduced the Iranian court ceremonies of sijda and paibos to emphasise that the nobles were not his equals.
2. He finally succeeded in breaking the power of the Chahalgani (the 'Forty', a faction of Turkish nobles).
3. He gave up wine and refused to laugh or joke in open court, to preserve the awe and dignity of the crown.
4. His chosen heir, Bughra Khan, was killed fighting the Mongols before he could ascend the throne.
Which of the above statements are correct?
a) 1, 2, 3 and 4
b) 1, 2 and 3 only
c) 2, 3 and 4 only
d) 1 and 2 only

**Correct Option: (b)**

**Justification:**
**Statement 1: Correct.** To emphasise that the nobles were not his equals, Balban insisted on the ceremony of sijada and paibos (prostration and kissing the monarch's feet). These ceremonies were Iranian in origin (Satish Chandra, History of Medieval India, PDF p. 99).
**Statement 2: Correct.** Balban was determined to break the power of the chahalgani, the Turkish nobles, and to exalt the power and prestige of the monarchy (Satish Chandra, PDF p. 98). The Karnataka text records that he controlled the disobedient Turkish sardars (DSERT Class 9 Social Science Part 1, 2026-27, p. 15).
**Statement 3: Correct.** Balban refused to laugh and joke in the court, and even gave up drinking wine so that no one might see him in a non-serious mood (Satish Chandra, PDF p. 99).
**Statement 4: Incorrect.** Bughra Khan was not killed. Balban's chosen successor, another son, had died earlier in a battle with the Mongols (Satish Chandra names him Prince Mahmud). Bughra Khan, the governor of Bengal, preferred to rule Bengal and Bihar rather than take the throne of Delhi (Satish Chandra, PDF pp. 105, 108).
Statements 1, 2 and 3 are correct, which is option (b). Options (a) and (c) include the false statement 4; option (d) omits the true statement 3.
Hence the correct answer is option (b).

**Result:** Balban's court ceremony, his curbing of the Turkish nobles and his studied gravity are all correct; Bughra Khan survived and kept Bengal.

**Salient Features: Balban**
Balban rose from slave to Sultan and spent his reign raising the authority of the crown.
- **Rise:** Balban was a slave of Iltutmish, and in Raziya's court he was appointed Amir-i-Shikar (DSERT Class 9 Social Science Part 1, 2026-27, p. 15). As Ulugh Khan he took the title naib-i-mulk, Deputy of the Realm, and became Sultan in 1266 (Tamil Nadu Class 11 History Vol 1, p. 166).
- **Justice:** to win public confidence, he administered justice with extreme impartiality (Satish Chandra, PDF p. 98).
- **Mongol frontier:** he strengthened the frontier by garrisoning forts at Bhatinda, Sunam and Samana (Tamil Nadu Class 11 History Vol 1, p. 168).
- **Bengal:** after Balban's death Bughra Khan assumed independence in Bengal and set up a dynasty that ruled there for the next forty years (Satish Chandra, PDF p. 105).

**Exam Fact:** Balban's campaign against Tughril in Bengal, which lasted three years, was the only distant campaign he undertook (Satish Chandra, History of Medieval India, PDF p. 105).

**9.** Which one of the following was NOT among the factors that helped Magadha become the most powerful mahajanapada between the sixth and fourth centuries BCE?
a) The river Indus as its main route of communication
b) Access to iron mines in present-day Jharkhand
c) Especially productive agriculture
d) Elephants from its forests for the army

**Correct Option: (a)**

**Justification:**
**Option (a): Correct.** This is the factor that was NOT among them. Magadha's cheap and convenient communication came from **the Ganga and its tributaries**, not the Indus (NCERT Class 12 History Part I, Ch 2, PDF p. 4).
**Option (b): Incorrect.** This was a factor, so it is not the answer: iron mines in present-day Jharkhand were accessible and provided resources for tools and weapons (NCERT Class 12 History Part I, Ch 2, PDF p. 4).
**Option (c): Incorrect.** This was a factor, so it is not the answer: Magadha was a region where agriculture was especially productive (NCERT Class 12 History Part I, Ch 2, PDF p. 4).
**Option (d): Incorrect.** This was a factor, so it is not the answer: elephants, an important part of the army, were found in the forests of the region (NCERT Class 12 History Part I, Ch 2, PDF p. 4).
Hence the correct answer is option (a).

**Result:** Agriculture, iron, elephants and the Ganga explain Magadha's rise; the Indus played no part.

**Salient Features: How Magadha became the most powerful mahajanapada**
Between the sixth and fourth centuries BCE, Magadha (in present-day Bihar) became the most powerful mahajanapada (NCERT Class 12 History Part I, Ch 2, PDF p. 4).
- **Two explanations:** modern historians point to resources and geography. Early Buddhist and Jaina writers credited ambitious kings and the ministers who carried out their policies.
- **Kings named in those writings:** Bimbisara, Ajatasattu and Mahapadma Nanda are the best known (NCERT Class 12 History Part I, Ch 2, PDF p. 4).
- **Early capital:** Rajagaha was a fortified settlement set among hills. Its name means "house of the king" (NCERT Class 12 History Part I, Ch 2, PDF p. 4).
- **Ganas or sanghas:** not every mahajanapada had a king. Some were oligarchies in which power was shared by a number of men, often called rajas, and both Mahavira and the Buddha belonged to such ganas (NCERT Class 12 History Part I, Ch 2, PDF p. 2).

**Exam Fact:** Rajagaha, the first capital of Magadha, is the Prakrit name for present-day Rajgir in Bihar (NCERT Class 12 History Part I, Ch 2, PDF p. 4).

**10.** Match the Vedic/ancient river name in List I with its identification in List II:

| List I | List II |
|---|---|
| A. Sindhu | I. A river of Afghanistan, today's Kabul river |
| B. Sarasvati | II. Identified with the now-vanished Ghaggar-Hakra river |
| C. Kubha | III. Same name retained; a tributary of the Ganga |
| D. Yamuna | IV. Same river, today called the Indus |

Which of the following is the correct combination?
a) A-IV, B-II, C-I, D-III
b) A-II, B-IV, C-III, D-I
c) A-IV, B-III, C-I, D-II
d) A-I, B-II, C-IV, D-III

**Correct Option: (a)**

**Justification:**
The correct pairing is A-IV, B-II, C-I, D-III.
- **Sindhu:** the Sindhu, identical with the Indus, is the river par excellence of the Rig Vedic people and is repeatedly mentioned (R.S. Sharma, Ancient India, p. 45). Sindhu–IV.
- **Sarasvati:** it is now lost in the sands of Rajasthan, and the area it represented is covered by the Ghaggar river (R.S. Sharma, p. 45). The Ghaggar-Hakra, which disappeared in the Thar desert, is believed to be the ancient Saraswathi (DSERT Class 6 Social Science Part 1, 2026-27, p. 75). Sarasvati–II.
- **Kubha:** rivers of Afghanistan such as the Kubha are mentioned in the Rig Veda (R.S. Sharma, p. 45). It is today's Kabul river. Kubha–I.

**11.** With reference to the administration of Shivaji, which one of the following statements is NOT correct?
a) Chauth was a levy of one-fourth of the land revenue on neighbouring Mughal territories.
b) Sardeshmukhi was an additional levy of ten per cent of the revenue.
c) The Pandit Rao among the Ashtapradhans was in charge of religious matters and donations.
d) The Peshwa, the prime minister, was also known by the Persian title Sar-i-Navbat.

**Correct Option: (d)**

**Justification:**
**Option (a): Incorrect.** The statement is accurate, so it is not the answer. Shivaji supplemented his income by levying a contribution on the neighbouring Mughal territories which came to one-fourth of the land revenue, and so was called Chauthai or Chauth (Satish Chandra, Medieval India Part II, PDF p. 181). II PUC History adds that in return he provided military protection to these areas (II PUC History, Ch 5.3, PDF p. 138).
**Option (b): Incorrect.** The statement is accurate. Sardeshmukhi was an additional levy of 10% that the ryots paid directly to Shivaji's central administration, over and above their regular taxes to local revenue officers (II PUC History, Ch 5.3, PDF p. 138; Satish Chandra, Medieval India Part II, PDF p. 181).
**Option (c): Incorrect.** The statement is accurate. Among the Ashtapradhans, the Pandit Rao, also called Danadhyaksha, was in charge of religious matters and donations (II PUC History, Ch 5.3, PDF p. 137). Satish Chandra likewise places the panditrao in charge of charitable grants (Satish Chandra, Medieval India Part II, PDF p. 179).

The Peshwa was the prime minister, but the Persian title Sar-i-Navbat belonged to the **Senapati**, the commander-in-chief of the army (II PUC History, Ch 5.3, PDF p. 137; Satish Chandra, Medieval India Part II, PDF p. 179, "sar-i-naubat (senapati)").
Hence the correct answer is option (d).

**Salient Features: Shivaji's administration**
Shivaji's system of administration was largely borrowed from the administrative practices of the Deccani states (Satish Chandra, Medieval India Part II, PDF p. 179).
- **Land revenue:** Shivaji classified land according to its fertility and yield. The revenue was fixed at 30% of the produce and later raised to 40%, payable in cash or in kind, and it was remitted in times of famine or other natural calamity (II PUC History, Ch 5.3, PDF p. 138).

- **Basis of the two levies:** Chauth was not a new feature, since zamindars in Gujarat had been charging banth, one-fourth (Chauthai) of the revenue, in areas under their control. The claim to sardeshmukhi rested on the notion that Shivaji was the head of all the deshmukhs in the area (Satish Chandra, Medieval India Part II, PDF p. 181).
- **Provinces and pay:** the kingdom was divided into four provinces, each under a viceroy called the Sardeshmukh; districts (desh) were headed by a Deshmukh or Deshpande and paraganas by a Desai. Shivaji stopped granting jagirs to his officers and paid their salaries in cash (II PUC History, Ch 5.3, PDF p. 138).

**Exam Fact:** When Shivaji ascended the gaddi, he put an impost called singhasanapatti on all the deshmukhs (Satish Chandra, Medieval India Part II, PDF p. 181).
